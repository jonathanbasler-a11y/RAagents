## 3. Proposed layer model (my proposal, for the team to decide)

**The honest answer to "5 or 6" is that the team named six things.** I propose counting five layers and drawing people as a frame around them, which is how the speaker introduced them ("overall there's the human role", L203). If the team prefers "six layers", draw the human layer as a vertical band beside the other five; the content is the same either way.

| # | Layer (bottom to top) | What it holds | Human owner | Why it is a layer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trust and governance | Decision rights, data rules, policies, privacy, global guardrails (L65), data walls, the audit record | Governance lead, with BD, Legal and data protection (proposed) | Set once for every playbook, so no use case ever needs its own trust rules. |
| 2 | Evidence and knowledge | Verified knowledge base, the standard finding format, provenance tags | Knowledge owner (proposed; not in the brief) | "No source, no finding": every agent reads from and writes to the same checkable base. |
| 3 | Agent library | Specialist agents (domain, team-role, stakeholder-perspective), each with skills, persona, its own guardrails and autonomy level | One agent owner per agent | One agent serves many playbooks, so a new capability needs no playbook change (L69). |
| 4 | Orchestration | The orchestrator and the standard workflow: plan, assign, assess, challenge, combine, escalate, a person decides, learn | The activator, with a platform owner (proposed) | How the team works is built once; each playbook says what to do, not how to run it. |
| 5 | Playbooks | One per use case: key questions, agents, sources, outputs, decisions reserved for people, autonomy ceiling, stakeholder identification (L219) | Playbook owner, who approves every update | The only layer a new use case adds; this is where the scalability claim stands or falls. |

**The frame: human accountability.** It holds the agent owners, playbook owners, decision owners, the activator, and the domain experts working each case. The decisions reserved for people sit here: deal-breakers, the overall assessment and the recommendation. **(my proposal)** An executive sponsor appoints the governance lead, so someone sits above the frame; that belongs in the ask to stakeholders.

**Rules that make it hold (my proposal)**
- **Autonomy is a dial, not a layer.** Each agent earns a level per playbook, and the playbook sets the ceiling (DD 2, inspections 1). The governance lead grants upgrades based on evidence from performance monitoring.
- **A person confirms the challenges that count.** A red-team agent can challenge any finding. But any finding that feeds a reserved decision or a PTRS input must be confirmed by a named person from the responsible function. This fixes today's worst behaviour: answering without the responsible function (CLAUDE.md:81).
- **Data walls cover learning too.** Lessons from a walled DD stay inside it, unless they are cleaned of case detail and a named person approves sharing them.
- **One audit record per finding:** playbook and agent versions, model and version, sources with retrieval dates, prompts and outputs, and the human sign-off.
- **Agents never cite themselves.** Agent-drafted text is tagged as such and never becomes evidence until a person verifies it.
- **The orchestrator is the engine of layer 4,** not one of the 6–8 specialist agents.

**Scalability test:** a new use case adds a playbook and a few agents, and layers 1, 2 and 4 stay unchanged (CLAUDE.md:41). So far this has been tested only on RA use cases; the brief also wants other functions (CLAUDE.md:23).

**Gaps for the team:**
- nobody yet owns trust, evidence or orchestration
- there is no agreed way to monitor agent performance ("I don't know what that looks like", L213)
- the effort to maintain agents isn't assigned or estimated (L241)

**Demo design (my proposal)**
- **The "blind" test may not be blind.** A well-known asset's outcome is probably in the model's training data. Before choosing, ask the model, with no documents, what happened to the asset. If it knows, pick another asset or say so on stage. Prefer an outcome after the model's training cutoff.
- **Pre-outcome sources are thinner than the brief's list.** FDA review documents and EPARs are usually published at or after the decision, so they can't go into a pre-outcome evidence pack. Check each source's date before choosing the asset.
- **Freeze the rubric before any run:** the issues to find and what counts as a hit. Report the hit rate across several runs, misses included.
- **On screen:**
  - the structured briefing, not buy/don't buy
  - one finding in full, with a clickable public source
  - the red team downgrading a weak finding
  - a visible pause where a person decides
- **Data walls can't really be shown with public data.** Show them as configuration (two different read-in lists), not as enforcement.
- **Timing:** keep the live part to 3–5 minutes, pre-run the long retrieval, and have the recorded fallback queued.

**New requirement: two colleagues at your company run the agents (my proposal)**
- **Your key never goes into the repo or into an app.** The repo is public and keys get scraped within minutes; a key inside a downloaded app can be extracted.
- **"The app" can be the repository itself:** the subagents in `.claude/agents/`, the playbook and the evidence pack. Anyone can download a public repo without an account. The colleagues' company-managed GitHub accounts can read it but can't contribute, so their changes come back through you.
- **Each colleague signs in with their own credential,** ideally access provided by the company, such as a company AI gateway or licence. A personal subscription used for company work may breach policy just as sharing your key would; check with IT first.
- **Not recommended:** sharing your key in any form. There is no per-person attribution or revocation, and it is likely against the provider's terms or company policy. A hosted proxy that holds your key is possible, but it means new code and hosting that the brief doesn't include.
- **Fallback:** replay mode, where the colleagues present a recorded run with no live LLM.
- The dev container in the repo is a bare Ubuntu image; it doesn't yet give the colleagues a working setup.
